Before Aldus PageMaker, before PostScript, before anyone said the words "desktop publishing" without laughing, there was a Xerox 914 in the back of a copy shop and a kid with a glue stick who could not afford the counter price. Everything you take for granted about the modern web interface, the fragment, the cut-out, the hyperlinked collage of unrelated blocks, was invented there, at five pence a sheet.

## The mimeograph as protocol

The mimeograph enforced constraints that read today like a style guide: one colour, usually purple; a fixed page size; type that had to be cut into a stencil, so every letter was a commitment. The result was a document with a *grammar*. You knew a mimeographed sheet was urgent because nobody mimeographed anything that wasn't.

The photocopier loosened the grammar and kept the urgency. Suddenly a photograph could be halftoned by accident, a headline could be ransom-noted from three different magazines, and the smudge from a jammed drum was as much a part of the page as the words. The machine collaborated.

## Paste-up is layout

Consider what a paste-up artist actually did. They took blocks of text, images, and rules, and arranged them on a board with hot wax. Blocks could be moved. Blocks could overlap. Blocks could be rotated one and a half degrees because the artist's hand was tired. Every one of those affordances is a CSS property now, and every one of them was a *physical* decision before it was a digital one.

The modern grid is a paste-up board with the wax removed. We put the wax back.

> Cheap toner was our Gutenberg press. If the machine jammed, the resulting black smudge became the cover design.
> <cite>— Anonymous paste-up editor, 1977</cite>

---

The jams, the smudges, the greyed-out photographs that came out of a tired drum: these were not failures of reproduction. They were reproduction *admitting it had happened*. That admission is the whole aesthetic we are defending in this folio.
